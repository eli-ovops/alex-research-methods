L62: Covered Calls Uncovered
L63: Many investors seek to protect their portfolios by purchasing equity index options. As a result, options tend to include a risk premium as a form of compensation to option sellers. Covered calls, which are short options, collect this volatility risk premium in addition to the equity risk premium earned from their long equity exposure. Because of option convexity, covered calls also embed active equity exposure that behaves like a reversal strategy.
L64: The authors contend that covered calls are rarely considered in terms of their risk exposures. In this paper, they introduce a novel performance attribution methodology that decomposes the strategy’s return into its passive and active equity and short volatility exposures.
L65: Not only does the performance attribution of their samples allow covered call investors to better understand the strategy’s characteristics, but it also allows portfolio managers to assess the risk and return impact of portfolio construction decisions, such as the call option’s strike and maturity, so that they may improve their strategy.
L66: As an example, the paper proposes a risk-managed covered call to hedge away the uncompensated active equity exposure, which the authors say is a significant contributor to the covered call’s risk. They contend that their proposed strategy has similar expected returns to the original covered call, but with lower risk, lower downside beta, and a higher Sharpe ratio.
L67: They added that while the motivation for covered calls is often confusing and muddled by a number of myths, the motivation for the risk-managed covered call is clear: earn the equity and volatility risk premium by constructing a portfolio with long equity and short volatility exposure.

L107@P1: ■ Discussion of findings. We demonstrate our
L108@P1: proposed performance attribution by analyzing
L109@P1: and comparing two covered call strategies. The
L110@P1: first strategy mimics the CBOE S&P 500 BuyWrite
L111@P1: Index (BXM), selling one-month at-the-money
L112@P1: call options on option expiration dates. The second strategy mimics the CBOE S&P 500 2% OTM
L113@P1: BuyWrite Index (BXY), selling one-month 2% outof-the-money call options on option expiration
L114@P1: dates. Our performance attribution shows that
L115@P1: passive equity is the dominant exposure for both
L116@P1: covered call strategies. Short volatility contributes
L117@P1: less than 10% of the risk but, with a Sharpe ratio
L118@P1: near 1.0, adds approximately 2% annualized return
L119@P1: to the covered call strategies.
L120@P1: Option-savvy market participants, such as
L121@P1: market makers, are well aware that options include
L122@P1: market timing, an active equity exposure. In fact,
L123@P1: they often use a delta-hedging program specifically
L124@P1: designed to reduce the risk arising from this dynamic
L125@P1: exposure. The covered call benchmark (CBOE S&P
L126@P1: 500 BuyWrite Index) and most covered call funds,
L127@P1: however, do not hedge the time-varying equity exposure arising from option convexity. Further, the risk
L128@P1: and return contribution of an unhedged short option
L129@P1: position’s dynamic equity exposure is by and large
L130@P1: not reported by those who manage to those who
L131@P1: invest in covered call strategies and is unaddressed
L132@P1: in the covered call literature.

L134@P1: one-quarter of the at-the-money covered call’s risk.
L135@P1: The timing bet is smallest immediately after option
L136@P1: expiration and largest just prior to option expiration.
L137@P1: In fact, on the day before the call option expires, the
L138@P1: equity timing position provides on average nearly
L139@P1: the same risk as the passive equity exposure. We
L140@P1: further show that covered call investors have not

L148@P1: the volatility risk premium.

L153@P1: day, the covered call’s active equity exposure may

L163@P1: volatility risk premiums but is no longer exposed to

L165@P1: to 0.52 by reducing its annualized volatility from
L166@P1: 11.4% to 9.2%.

L267@P3: passive (strategic) and active (tactical) components.
L268@P3: Its exposure to realized volatility (gamma) fluctuates over time. Gamma is higher when the option is
L269@P3: close to the money and close to its expiration. Short
L270@P3: volatility’s exposure to changes in implied volatility
L271@P3: (vega) also fluctuates over time. Vega is higher when
L272@P3: the option is close to the money and distant from its
L273@P3: expiration. In fact, short volatility can be split across
L274@P3: other related dimensions as well. The option’s maturity represents a calendar bet, and the option’s strike
L275@P3: price represents a skew bet. Decomposing across all
L276@P3: these dimensions may be tractable if there is a welldefined passive short volatility asset. Unfortunately,
L277@P3: there is not, and we do not attempt to define one
L278@P3: in this article. For this reason, and to maintain parsimony in our performance attribution, we do not
L279@P3: further decompose short volatility across any of the
L280@P3: previously identified dimensions.
L281@P3: An alternative performance attribution could
L282@P3: regress covered call returns on the S&P 500 Index
L283@P3: return and an S&P 500 variance swap return. The
L284@P3: regression framework is commonly used to estimate
L285@P3: return exposures to known factors. Rolling estimation of factor coefficients provides information on
L286@P3: exposure dynamics. We believe our attribution methodology is more appropriate for a covered call for
L287@P3: the following reasons. First, we know the covered
L288@P3: call’s constituent assets, so we may use a model to
L289@P3: estimate equity exposure at a point in time rather
L290@P3: than statistically estimate an average exposure over
L291@P3: a rolling period. Option convexity can lead to rapid
L292@P3: changes in equity exposure, and the regression will
L293@P3: not be able to fully capture these changes and thus
L294@P3: will underestimate the equity exposure dynamics. Second, there is no well-defined pure passive
L295@P3: short volatility return series. A variance swap is one
L296@P3: method to obtain short volatility exposure. Delta
L297@P3: hedging a short option is another method. Shorting
L298@P3: CBOE Volatility Index futures is a third, and so on.
L299@P3: The delta-hedged option has exposure to a variance
L300@P3: swap but with basis risk. That basis risk would be an
L301@P3: additional term that complicates the decomposition.
L302@P3: Our performance attribution mechanically decomposes the covered call’s return using a model into
L303@P3: passive equity, the delta-hedged option variant of
L304@P3: short volatility, and equity market timing.
