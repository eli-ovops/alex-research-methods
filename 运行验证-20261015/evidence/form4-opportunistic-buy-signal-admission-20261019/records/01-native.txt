## Introduction

The trading behaviour of corporate insiders has been of ongoing interest to investors, regulators, and scholars. Having preferential access to non-public information of the firms they work for, insiders are often incentivised to trade on such advantage for profit (e.g., Seyhun, 1986; Meulbroek, 1992; Lakonishok and Lee, 2001; Cheng and Lo, 2006). Insiders also facilitate price discovery when new information is incorporated into price through insider trading (e.g., Kyle, 1985). While insiders are considered as superiorly informed traders on average, extant studies find that they are not a homogenous group that is equally informed. For example, Cohen et al. (2012) show that the trades of “routine” insiders (those having a consistent historical trading pattern) are not as informed as those of “non-routine” or “opportunistic” insiders. Similarly, Akbas, Jiang, and Koch (2020, hereafter AJK) define a new measure, namely investment horizon, to identify superiorly informed insiders. They find that short-horizon insiders, who frequently trade to update their positions, realize profits in a timelier manner than long-horizon insiders. AJK show that short-horizon insider trades convey more value-relevant private information than long-horizon trades for up to 12 months following insider trades.

Despite extant research indicating the presence of material information in insider investment horizons, evidence is largely limited to the U.S. context. As the trading activity of corporate insiders is generally under more stringent regulation requirements than that of other investors, we believe a country's legal system governing insiders and shaping the institutional environment is an important factor underpinning the trading behaviour of insiders. This has arguably made the monitoring of insider trading in AU more difficult than in the U.S. It also results in different trading behaviours of AU insiders as they can delay the disclosure of transactions for longer. Unlike U.S. insiders that are contrarian (Rozeff and Zaman, 1998; Lakonishok and Lee, 2001), AU insiders tend to exhibit a momentum trading pattern (Brown et al., 2003). Furthermore, the AU stock market contains many small illiquid stocks compared to the U.S. market, which deters insiders from stock trading due to higher transaction costs (Hodgson and Van Praag, 2006). Overall, a study of the information content of AU insider investment horizons may provide different insights from those of AJK.

Based on a sample of AU insider trades from 2002 to 2021, we find that, consistent with the insider trading literature, AU insiders have superior information to other traders regarding future returns. However, contrary to their counterparts in the U.S., AU insider investment horizons do not contain non-public information.

Based on a sample of AU insider trades from 2002 to 2021, we find that, consistent with the insider trading literature, AU insiders have superior information to other traders regarding future returns. However, contrary to their counterparts in the U.S., AU insider investment horizons do not contain non-public information. Hence, unlike in the U.S. market, an investment horizon is not an indicator of insiders' private information in the AU market. In addition, we also show that the majority of AU insiders are buyers while the majority of U.S. insiders are sellers. We believe that the different results in the AU market are driven by the lower litigation risks and the distinct trading behaviours of AU insiders.

We conduct the first study on the information content of AU insider investment horizons, implicitly incorporating the influence of the legal environment surrounding the trading activity of insiders. We add to the insider trading literature by exploring the investment horizons of AU insiders. Although AJK document that short-horizon insider trades convey more private information than long-horizon insider trades in the U.S. market, such results cannot be generalised to the AU market because the insiders of the two counties have distinct trading patterns and are subject to different legal requirements.

Decoding Inside Information

Article

  * Oct 2010
  * J FINANC

  * Lauren Cohen
  * Christopher J. Malloy
  * Lukasz Pomorski

Using a simple empirical strategy, we decode the information in insider trades. Exploiting the fact that insiders trade for a variety of reasons, we show that there is predictable, identifiable “routine” insider trading that is not informative for the future of firms. Stripping away these routine trades, which comprise over half the entire universe of insider trades, leaves a set of information-rich “opportunistic” trades that contains all the predictive power in the insider trading universe. A portfolio strategy that focuses solely on opportunistic insider trades yields value-weight abnormal returns of 82 basis points per month, while the abnormal returns associated with routine traders are essentially zero. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------
